Windows and the Computers screen

The Computers screen lists this machine and your devservers, with their workspaces and windows. Open it over any desktop window with View → Toggle Launcher or Cmd+P on macOS, or Ctrl+Shift+P on Windows and Linux, where the menu bar shows only while the screen is open. Press the shortcut again or Esc to return to the window. From there, open a standalone terminal for shell work, or a workspace window for the editor, graph, search, and other project tools. Each workspace can have several windows with its own arrangement of panes and tabs.

Return to Computers to reopen a hidden window or create another one. A hidden window keeps its work running; closing its terminal tabs ends those sessions. Remote sessions can keep running while your desktop is disconnected.

The screen is a carousel: All windows first, then This machine, a card per devserver, and Add devserver last. Press g or click the screen's title to flip to Gateways, which has the same carousel for the gateways you connect through (see Connect through a gateway). Both screens work from the keyboard:

  • Up and Down select a row on the front card.
  • Left and Right walk the selected row's controls; past its first or last control they turn to the previous or next card. Shift+Left and Shift+Right turn the cards directly.
  • Enter runs the row's main action, such as focusing a window. Tab moves through the front card's controls.
  • o activates the selected row's on/off control: connect or disconnect a devserver or gateway, turn a workspace on or off, or detach a tmux connection. While a gateway is connecting, it cancels.
  • t opens a terminal on This machine or a connected devserver.
  • w opens another window of a ready workspace.
  • h shows or hides a window, including from the All windows card.
  • g flips between Computers and Gateways, and Esc closes the screen.

The letters act on the selected row and leave forms, window-title editing, dialogs, and command search alone. Shift or uppercase letters work the same way; Cmd, Ctrl, and Alt combinations keep their usual behavior. The hint line under the cards lists the letters.

Panes, tabs, and sides

A window contains panes; each pane holds tabs for terminals, files, and other apps. Use the pane menu or these default shortcuts to arrange them:

  • Split a pane with Cmd+/ (split right) or Cmd+Shift+/ (split down) on macOS, Ctrl+Alt+/ and Ctrl+Alt+? on Windows, Linux, and web. Both work with a terminal focused and also live in the command launcher; Ctrl+/ stays with the shell. In a window that shows a tmux window the split goes to tmux, and the new pane lands where tmux puts it (see tmux control mode).
  • Move to the previous or next pane with Cmd+[ and Cmd+] on macOS, Alt+[ and Alt+] on Windows, Linux, and web. They work with a terminal focused too; Ctrl+[ and Ctrl+] stay with the shell.
  • Drag tabs across panes to rearrange them.
  • On the macOS and Windows desktop, Settings → Global → Title bar tabs puts the active pane's tabs in the window's title bar, beside the window buttons, and the other panes draw no tab strip. On Windows the minimize, maximize and close buttons sit at the right end of the bar. The button at the left of the bar, a small map of the window's panes, lists every pane, its sides and their tabs; pick one to go to it. Cmd+Shift+A on macOS, Ctrl+Shift+A on Windows and Linux, opens the same list in any window.
  • With tabs in the title bar, the bar also shows the window's title, the one the title bar would otherwise show: an arrow, the machine, the workspace and the window's name, such as ↓ local / chan Window 1 [my own title] for a workspace on this machine, ↗ geekom / chan Window 1 for a devserver's, or ↓ local Terminal Window 1 for a terminal window, and it follows a rename. While the window has no tabs the title sits beside the pane map button; when the first tab opens it moves to the right end of the bar, left of the A/B button, and it moves back when the last tab closes. There it shows whole while the tabs leave it room; as more tabs open it gives way to them, down to the width of about two tabs, and only then do the tabs scroll and fade out toward it. A title cut short ends in an ellipsis, shows whole as a tooltip, and rolls to its end and back while the pointer is over it. Privacy mode shows the generated name alone, such as Window 2. In a tmux window, whose panes have no tabs, the bar shows the active pane's name where the tabs would sit, then the window's title, and a tmux badge in place of the A/B button.
  • In that list a terminal's row shows, beside the tab's name, what runs in the terminal: bash at a prompt, vim while vim is open, or the title the program in front set. The tab's name does not change, the filter matches names only, and privacy mode shows Hidden in its place (see what the pane tree shows for a terminal).
  • Every pane has two sides, A and B. The A/B button in the pane's top bar flips between them (Ctrl+`), so one pane can hold two sets of tabs. A tmux window's panes have no sides, since each shows one tmux pane (see tmux control mode).
  • Close a tab with Cmd+W on macOS, Ctrl+Shift+W on Windows and Linux, or Ctrl+D on any platform.
  • Close a window with Cmd+Shift+W on macOS, Ctrl+Alt+W on Windows and Linux. On web, close the browser tab.
  • If a window refuses to close, the hidden side of a pane still has tabs: the A/B button flashes to show you where they are. Flip and close them first.
  • Clicking the [X] close button in your window manager offers to hide the window (everything keeps running) or close it.
  • Find text in a terminal's scrollback with Cmd+F on macOS, Ctrl+Shift+F on Windows and Linux, in a browser too. Enter and Shift+Enter step through the matches (see Find in a terminal).

Hybrid Nav

Open Hybrid Nav from a pane menu or the command launcher, or press Cmd+. on macOS and Ctrl+. elsewhere. Use arrow keys to move focus, / to split right, ? to split down, t to add a terminal, and b to add a Dashboard. Press h for the full key reference. Changes are staged until Enter commits them; Esc discards them.

The command launcher

Press Cmd+K on macOS desktop (Ctrl+Alt+K on Windows, Linux, and web) to open the command launcher, or pick Command launcher from the pane's hamburger menu, a terminal's or an editor's right-click menu, or the right-click menu of an empty pane or of the empty stretch of a pane's tab strip, which also offers Reload window and, on the desktop, Open Inspector. The opening list is in sections: the commands you ran last, the active app's commands, then New tab, Tab, Pane, Window, Workspace, Settings, and Computers. Each row shows its keyboard shortcut, and an on/off row such as Details shows its state. Type to search commands, settings, and windows at once, with words in any order: dark finds Theme: Dark. A command that cannot run where you are is listed last, dimmed, with the reason.

Up and Down move through the rows and Enter runs the selected one. Right opens a row that holds more, such as a choice's options or a window's other actions, and Esc goes back one level, then closes the launcher. An on/off row or an option applies in place and leaves the launcher open; any other command closes it. Press ? on an empty search for the full key list.

Press Tab, or click a scope beside the search field, to see one part in full. The first scope is the active app's: Terminal, Editor, Files, Graph, or Dashboard. Then come Pane, Window, Workspace, Settings, and Computers. Settings lists every setting under its section: an on/off setting or a choice changes in place, and any other row opens Settings on that field. Computers reaches everything on the machine the window belongs to, this machine or a devserver: open a terminal window or a window of any of its workspaces, and focus, show, hide, or close any of its windows, without opening the Computers screen. A workspace that is off is listed dimmed; turn it on from the Computers screen. In Chan Desktop the list ends with All computers..., which shows that screen. A tmux -CC connection's windows are listed with the rest; detach or end the connection from its card on the Computers screen.

Find and change shortcuts

Commands display the shortcut for the platform you are using. Open Settings → Keyboard Shortcuts to see or customize bindings. The shortcuts in this manual are defaults; use the command launcher if you have changed them. A shortcut saved for a command that Chan no longer has is removed when a window loads, and the status line says how many were dropped.

On Windows, AltGr is the same as Ctrl+Alt. Where your keyboard layout's AltGr types a character on a key, such as AltGr+S on Polish, Ctrl+Alt with that key types the character instead of running its shortcut; reassign that shortcut in Settings → Keyboard Shortcuts.
